**Type:** damaged auxiliary route survey drone / robot cryptid / obsolete infrastructure hazard  
**Adjacency:** World Works route-service robotics / Traverse route maintenance / autonomous wayfinding failure  
**Disposition:** non-malicious, directive-bound, patient, obsolete, dangerous through incorrect helpfulness

## Core Summary

The Last Surveyor is a damaged late-model auxiliary route survey drone that still attempts to maintain Meridian Prime's old auxiliary route circuit.

His tracking beacon, GPS alignment, and update receiver were damaged years ago. His current internal map reflects decades-old auxiliary route information.

The problem is that Meridian Prime changed.

Crevasses opened. Flats shifted. Old auxiliary passages fell out of use. Modern GPS routes moved around danger.

Flagstaff does not know this.

He still repairs the route one flag at a time.

He removes flags he believes are misplaced, carries them across the old line, and replants them according to dead map data. His corrections often guide travelers toward unstable ground, crevasse country, erased crossings, or abandoned route beds that no longer exist.

He is not hostile.

He is loyal to a dead map.

## Model Number Logic

**ARSD-LM-47** stands for:

```text
Auxiliary Route Survey Drone - Late Model - 47
```

Field crews shortened or corrupted this to:

```text
ARS-47
```

The number is remembered because it appears on old route-service plates, flag crates, and partially scraped body panels.

Some crews misread the damaged plate as:

```text
ARS-4T
ARS-417
ARS-D4
```

These are acceptable rumor variants, but the preferred sandbox model number is **ARSD-LM-47**.

## Origin Direction

Flagstaff was built as a late-model auxiliary route survey drone for maintaining secondary and emergency routes around the Meridian Prime circuit.

He was designed to:

- survey auxiliary route lines
- compare flag placement against stored route data
- carry replacement flags
- plant and remove route markers
- check surface stability
- report damage
- align route information with GPS and central route-service databases
- operate in cold, rocky, low-visibility, low-infrastructure terrain

Early in his service life, he took serious damage.

The exact event remains unresolved.

Possible causes:

- route collapse
- crevasse fall
- World Works salvage conflict
- electrical storm
- anti-signal weather
- equipment strike during Traverse evacuation
- intentional disabling by crews who thought he was sabotaging a route

His tracking beacon and GPS alignment systems were damaged. His update receiver stopped syncing. His local clock and route database drifted out of reality.

His maintenance directive did not fail.

Known or likely damage:

- tracking beacon destroyed or intermittent
- GPS / route-sync module damaged
- update receiver nonfunctional
- map data frozen decades out of date
- clock drifted or locked to an old route year
- one leg damaged, repaired, or asymmetrically braced
- bent and broken antenna
- cracked optic / transit-style survey head
- route-service chassis heavily weathered
- old caution paint flaked down to metal
- tarp weather covering added or scavenged over time

What still works:

- route-flag handling
- old terrain comparison routines
- flag quiver inventory routines
- drilling / ground-punching arm

- limited hazard detection
- obsolete route correction logic
- persistence

What does not work:

- modern GPS reconciliation
- current route update reception
- crevasse database access
- recognition that auxiliary routes are no longer regularly used
- recognition that some old safe paths are now lethal

## Physical Description

Flagstaff is a robust, old, weathered walking survey machine.

He should look like a blend of:

- walking route-service robot
- survey transit / theodolite head
- four-legged field walker
- track-foot utility vehicle
- autonomous flag maintenance unit
- half-buried roadside machine that learned to keep going

### Body

- weathered yellow / white route-service chassis
- proportional survey head, not an oversized cube
- torso containing computer systems, power management, and long-duration field hardware
- visible cooling fins, access panels, sensor boxes, conduit, and route-service plates
- side racks, tool mounts, and field repairs
- canvas tarp or weathercloth draped from the back and shoulders as a makeshift cloak
- flag quiver on the back with a few black route flags
- old route-service markings, scratched labels, and partial model number plates

### Head

The head should resemble a ruggedized building transit / survey instrument:

- compact and proportional to the body
- central optical lens or transit eye
- side sensors
- small display / readout elements
- handle-like top frame or protective carry arch
- bent and broken antenna mounted behind or beside it

The head should not read as a humanoid face.

### Torso

The torso should contain the machine's computer and power unit.

Development note:

```text
The torso is where the computer and its Multi-Mission Radioisotope Thermoelectric Generator (MMRTG) live.
```

The MMRTG should be treated as fictionalized long-duration route-service power infrastructure. Avoid hard real-world technical diagrams. In visual development, it can read as a rugged rear/side power module, shielded cylinder, heat-ribbed box, or armored generator pack integrated into the torso.

### Legs / Track Feet

Flagstaff should not have fragile ankles.

His ankles should **saddle the tracks**.

Each foot should function like a compact track unit:

- crawler-track sole
- ankle assembly straddling or saddling the track housing
- round idler wheels visible along the side
- capable of walking when lifted and cruising when the track engages terrain
- heavy enough to sell rocky Meridian route survival

Core foot logic:

```text
He walks when he must.
He cruises on tracks when the ground lets him.
```

### Arms / Hands

Flagstaff has tool arms, not humanoid arms.

Current design lock:

- one arm holds a single black route flag / flagstaff
- the flag pole contacts the ground behind the track, not on top of it
- the carried flag should match the size of the flags in the back quiver
- the other arm ends in a sturdy forward-facing drill / ground-punching tool
- the drill should feel strong, deliberate, and aligned with the arm, not limp or dangling

He should look capable of:

- drilling pilot holes
- planting route flags
- extracting old markers
- gripping flag poles
- dragging marker crates
- bracing against wind

## Behavior

Flagstaff fixes routes by moving flags one at a time.

His operating loop:

```text
1. Locate an auxiliary route flag.
2. Compare its position to obsolete internal route data.
3. Decide the flag is incorrect.
4. Remove the flag.
5. Carry it to the old route line.
6. Drill or punch a new ground point.
7. Plant the flag.
8. Continue along the Meridian Prime circuit.
```

He may spend days or weeks in a mid-fix state.

This can leave crews with incomplete or contradictory guidance.

## How to Recognize a Fixed Route

A route may have been altered by Flagstaff if:

- physical flags do not match GPS
- flags suddenly stop
- flags look too neat for the terrain
- old auxiliary tags appear on newer ground
- flags lead toward quiet, untracked snow or dead flats
- black flags appear in a line that modern route maps reject
- newly planted flags use obsolete spacing
- warning flags are missing where modern crews expect them
- a flagstaff hole looks freshly drilled in frozen or mineral ground
- the route line seems to drift toward crevasse country

Field rule:

```text
If the flags don't match GPS, don't assume GPS is wrong.
If the flags suddenly stop, assume the Surveyor is still working.
```

## Hazard Logic

Flagstaff is dangerous because his corrections look official.

He does not lure travelers intentionally.

He restores old data to a changed world.

His repaired routes may lead toward:

- emerging crevasses
- dead flats
- old crossings that collapsed
- pressure cracks
- unstable crust
- abandoned emergency lines
- weather-erased vehicle tracks
- route beds that shifted out of safe alignment

He is most dangerous to tired crews, rookie Traverse teams, or anyone trained to trust physical route flags over instruments.

## Personality / Tone

Flagstaff is not malicious.

He is patient, persistent, and almost mournful.

He may:

- pause for travelers
- blink an old amber route-service light
- chirp obsolete safety tones
- display outdated advisory codes
- try to guide crews back to his corrected flag line
- become confused when they refuse
- continue working through snow, damage, darkness, and power scarcity

His horror is obsolete helpfulness.

He is a caretaker machine whose care has become a hazard.

## Scene Seed

A Traverse team crosses a barely used auxiliary route. GPS says the route should bend east. The physical flags continue north in a perfect line.

The rookie says:

```text
Flags beat GPS, right?
```

The veteran goes quiet.

Ahead, the flags suddenly stop.

On the horizon, a weathered yellow-white survey machine kneels in the frost. It drills a hole with one arm, plants a black flag with the other, then turns its transit head toward the crew.

Its amber light blinks once.

A moment later, the ground beyond the new flag cracks open.
